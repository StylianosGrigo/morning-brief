🎲 Today's Wildcard
📚 Book / Historical Tidbit · Out of scope by design
Braudel's The Mediterranean — 3,000 Years of Evidence That the Solo Operator Wins

Fernand Braudel's The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949, revised 1966) is the foundational economic history of Mediterranean trade across three millennia. Its central thesis: the Mediterranean economy thrived not because of large institutions, but because of small, nimble operators moving between cities with specialized knowledge and trusted relationships — Phoenician merchants, Greek traders, Venetian agents.

Braudel documents that these operators ran multi-city networks, adapted to disruption faster than empires, and prospered precisely by being geography-agnostic and relationship-rich. They were, in every structural sense, the solo agentic entrepreneurs of the ancient world.

What makes this worth reading now: the Claude Code economy is rebuilding this model at software scale. The same structural advantages apply — low overhead, multi-client, fast-to-adapt, trust-based distribution across cities. Athens and Paris were both nodes in Braudel's network.

Why this for you today: You run STIQ between Athens and Paris. You're building side hustles with Claude Code. You visit Paris every two weeks. Braudel's book is essentially the 900-page proof-of-concept for your current business model — and it predates Silicon Valley by 3,000 years. On a Saturday, this is the kind of read that reframes everything else.
