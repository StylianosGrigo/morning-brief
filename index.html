The Suez Crisis (1956) and How It Built the Greek Shipping Empire — A Hormuz Mirror

When Egypt nationalized the Suez Canal in 1956 and the subsequent crisis closed it for months, it rerouted global trade around Africa — just like Hormuz is doing today. The diversion created massive demand for tanker capacity at a time when Greek shipping families (Onassis, Niarchos, Livanos) had the vessels and the nerve to profit. Aristotle Onassis doubled his tanker fleet in the 12 months following the Suez closure. Stavros Niarchos built the world's first supertanker specifically because the around-Africa route made large vessels economically superior. The Suez Crisis is widely credited as the moment the Greek shipping empire became genuinely global.

Today: Iran has mined the Hormuz Strait, forcing exactly the same around-Africa rerouting. Greek shipping still controls ~20% of global capacity. The playbook from 1956 is sitting in the archives — but the families who benefited are still there, and their successors are navigating the same geopolitical disruption pattern.

Why this for you today: You're watching the Hormuz crisis unfold as a daily news item. This reframes it as a recurring structural pattern in Mediterranean geopolitics — one that has historically created generational wealth for Greek operators who moved fast.
